How AI Is Powering Tomorrow’s Jaw-Dropping Graphics

When it comes to graphics processing and computation, gaming is often the bellwether for innovation. 

Think of it like this: if your game stutters or looks outdated, people notice. So game studios constantly push for better graphics, faster performance, etc., which leads to the creation of tech that spreads to other industries.

NVIDIA has long blazed a trail in gaming, popularizing the term ‘GPU’ (graphics processing unit) from about 1999. They ended up doing pretty well outside of gaming; maybe you heard. 

Now NVIDIA’s highly sought-after GeForce RTX GPUs are being used “to accelerate neural networks from within a game’s graphics pipeline,” as part of a partnership with Microsoft. 

Neural networks. Huh. 

Don’t worry, it ain’t as complex as it sounds. Let us explain a teeny bit of the tech:

  • CPU: central processing unit. Any computer’s brain, basically — even the earliest ones.

  • GPU: came later, originally developed for processing graphics. NVIDIA the main player. Main use case: gaming. 

  • NPU: neural processing unit, aka AI accelerator. Architected specifically for machine-learning tasks. Leverages neural networks. 

Back to NVIDIA. Its special GPUs use Tensor Cores (AI boosters), which do the same job an NPU would: run neural networks fast. 

Why this matters: Through their partnership, NVIDIA and Microsoft will use this hardware for neural shading (smarter lighting), a technology which “represents a revolution in graphics programming.” 

Honestly, it’s hard to argue with that. 

Neural shading uses AI to help decide how a scene should look, based on what it’s learned from visual data. Instead of using fixed rules for lighting and textures like traditional shaders, it learns patterns, like how light bounces or how materials behave, and fills in the details in a smarter, more realistic way. 

The result is better-looking graphics with less manual work, and often, better performance.

In a nutshell: Modern GPUs and chips are packing dedicated AI hardware — whether called Tensor Cores, NPUs, or some other fancy word — making things like neural shaders, DLSS, or AI image generation possible.
