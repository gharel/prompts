AI tech boom reveals global inequality: Anthropic data shows Singapore and Israel leading AI adoption, while emerging economies lag behind in the transformative tech race.

Welcome, humans.

AI adoption is outpacing every technology in history, but this speed is creating massive inequality gaps. Anthropic just released fascinating data showing that wealthy countries like Singapore and Israel are using Claude 4-7x more per capita than emerging economies. 

Even within the US, Washington DC and Utah are crushing California in per-capita usage. Countries with lower adoption rates use AI almost exclusively for coding, while high-adoption areas spread usage across education, science, and business.

See, the data reveals something striking: as AI capabilities improve, users in wealthy regions collaborate more with AI, while users in emerging economies just delegate complete tasks to it. In a way, we're watching different parts of the world develop entirely different relationships with AI technology.

OpenAI Board Chairman Says We're Living Through AI's Dot-com Moment (And That's Actually Good)

Remember when everyone called the dot-com boom a bubble, then watched Amazon and Google become trillion-dollar companies? OpenAI board chairman Bret Taylor thinks we're living through AI's version of 1999, and that's not necessarily bad news.

In a recent interview at The Verge, Taylor echoed Sam Altman's prediction that “someone is going to lose a phenomenal amount of money” in AI, but he's not sweating it. His take? We're absolutely in a bubble, but bubbles can birth the next generation of tech giants.

Here's his logic: The dot-com crash killed companies like Pets.com. But it also gave us Amazon, Google, and pretty much every major tech company that runs our world today. Even “failed” ideas later became successful companies like Instacart and DoorDash.

But Reddit users aren't buying it, saying that AI actually shouldn’t be compared to the dot-com bubble. The post pointed out that Pets.com had just $600K in revenue before imploding, while OpenAI just announced $10B in annual revenue. AI is already embedded across enterprise workflows, government systems, and education.

Without question, AI is genuinely being used across industries (just look at the Anthropic data in today’s blurb), but the profitability question remains thorny. And here's where it gets tricky. Despite massive revenues, major AI companies are still spending enormous amounts on compute infrastructure, top talent, and free user services. The costs of running these models at scale remain a significant challenge.

The key difference this time: Unlike the dot-com era where companies could burn cash for years chasing growth, today's AI boom demands faster results. Companies need to prove ROI quickly or risk getting cut off.

Here’s what this means for AI companies:

  • Some will become the next Amazon (massive winners).

  • Others will become the next Pets.com (spectacular failures).

  • The underlying AI infrastructure will get used, even if early builders go bankrupt.

  • Companies that can't show quick value will get weeded out faster.

Whether you buy the bubble comparison or not, one thing's clear: we're in a period where massive money is chasing AI opportunities. The companies that survive will be those building real solutions with clear paths to profitability, not just impressive demos.

Case in point: OpenAI just launched GPT-5-Codex, a specialized model built specifically for coding tasks that can work autonomously for over 7 hours on complex projects (and Every says you can get it to code for 35 minute straight “if you ask it nicely.”).

Here’s a few more details:

  • 90% faster task completion through improved cloud infrastructure.

  • Automatic code reviews that catch critical bugs other tools miss.

  • Dynamic thinking that's snappy for simple tasks but goes deep on complex code refactors (restructuring messy code to make it better without changing what it does).

The proof it's working? Codex usage grew 10x in the past month, with companies like Duolingo, Virgin Atlantic, and Cisco Meraki using it for real production work.

“Codex caught a real active bug that other code review tools missed,” says Austin Ray from Ramp. “Precision and value are high per caught bug.”
