😺 An AI-designed drug made patients look younger on six aging clocks

Okay, this is one of those studies where the headline sounds completely insane, and then the actual details are somehow still pretty wild.

Insilico Medicine used AI to design rentosertib, an experimental drug for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, a disease that scars the lungs.

Now researchers went back to blood samples from its 12-week Phase 2a trial and asked a completely different question:

Did the drug also make people look biologically younger?

Basically, they ran the samples through six different proteomic aging clocks, which estimate biological age by looking at patterns in the proteins floating around your blood.

Here’s what happened:

  • All six aging clocks consistently estimated lower biological ages in patients taking rentosertib.

  • The strongest effects showed up around week four, with some doses averaging roughly 3-4 years “younger.”

  • One clock measured an effect of roughly six years, while some artery-specific measurements moved even further.

  • Weirdly, the dose that looked best for biological aging was not the same dose that performed best on lung function.

So... did AI just invent an anti-aging pill?

What the researchers actually showed is narrower, but arguably more useful. These clocks cannot perfectly separate “your body is aging more slowly” from “your disease is improving and therefore your proteins look healthier.”

But the researchers also found changes in biological pathways associated with cellular aging and metabolism, alongside the expected anti-fibrotic effects.

And importantly, six different clocks all pointed the same way. Nobel laureate Michael Levitt argued that agreement may matter more than any exact “years younger” number because the clocks use different features and training data.

Our take: This might be less about discovering the fountain of youth and more about changing how we test drugs. Instead of running one trial for lung disease and another someday for longevity, researchers could measure both at once.

Insilico founder Alex Zhavoronkov called it “the most important paper in my life to date.”

Which, coming from the guy whose company literally uses AI to invent drugs, is saying something.

🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Build better 3D worlds with Astra

So GPT-6 Astra’s “Ghibli moment” might actually be Blender. Seriously, if you’ve seen one of the absurd Astra demos floating around lately, there’s a pretty good chance Blender is involved somewhere.

OpenAI has been showing Astra turning Blender models into playable worlds, and Matt Shumer has basically taken that idea and run directly toward “can I build GTA before GTA 6 comes out?” His increasingly ridiculous 3D experiments have now pulled 15M+ views.

So naturally, everyone keeps asking him the same thing: what prompt are you using?

Turns out, the crazy results are not coming from some magic prompt. Shumer says the real trick is the process around it.

His recipe is Reference → Assets → Assembly → Critique → Ship:

  1. Start with real photos, maps, or generated concept art so Astra has an exact visual target.

  2. Have specialized sub-agents build individual assets in Blender, then assemble them in Three.js or Unreal.

  3. Give a fresh agent the render and reference image blind. Every difference it spots becomes the builder’s next assignment.

  4. Keep looping until the critic can’t reliably tell which image is real.

For browser games, Shumer favors Three.js + Blender. For huge photoreal worlds, crowds, and characters, use Unreal.

🛠️ Tuesday Tool Tip

OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra hit 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3, one of the toughest AI reasoning tests out there. Then ARC Prize ran the exact same model with a stripped-down setup, and the score cratered to 62.7%. Same brain, different scaffolding, completely different result.

Here's the actual tip: stop trusting other people's benchmarks and build your own.

Pick one real task you already need done (a research report, a messy spreadsheet, an actual bug you're stuck on). Run it through Astra and whatever you use now, side by side. Then score both on four things:

  • Did it finish the job correctly?

  • How many times did you have to jump in and fix something?

  • How long did it take, start to finish?

  • What did it actually cost?

Our favorite insight: the model that finishes in 15 minutes but needs six corrections isn't necessarily the winner over the one that takes 30 and hands you something done.
