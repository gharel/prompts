😺 Google Opens Gemini 4 Argon to Paying Users and Ties OpenAI's Flagship

Google launched Gemini 4 Argon on Tuesday, two weeks after announcing it to a small group of security defenders. We flagged that early access on Oct. 2, when it was still limited to testers. Now Pro and Ultra subscribers can try it in the Gemini app, and paid API customers can build with it.

The number worth repeating is hallucination, meaning how often a model gives a wrong answer instead of admitting it doesn't know. On Artificial Analysis's AA-Omniscience test, Argon scored 15%, versus 51% for GPT-6 Astra and 54% for GPT-6.1 Sol.

The catch is accuracy. Argon got 50% of the questions right versus Astra's 63%, because that test punishes wrong answers but not skipped ones. So Argon wins partly by saying "I'm not sure" more often. Every office has that coworker, and most of us trust them more.

Here's how it stacks up against the field:

  • OpenAI: Argon ties GPT-6 Astra at 53 on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index and sits one point above GPT-6.1 Sol. Astra still wins some of Google's own rows, like FrontierSWE v2 (65.5% to 55.0%).

  • Anthropic: Claude Opus 5.5 still leads that index at 58, and Sonnet 5.5 sits at 56. Google's own table has Argon ahead of Opus 5.5 on DeepSWE, 77.9% to 74.2%.

  • xAI: Grok 4.7 scores about 46 on the same index, per one tracker, so Argon passes it.

  • China: Bloomberg Intelligence found, as we reported Oct. 5, that top Chinese models trail U.S. rivals by 3%, down from 15%. That reading predates Argon, so the next scorecard shows whether Google's comeback widens the gap again.

Price is Google's best argument. Argon costs $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens through Nov 30, then $4 and $20, matching Opus 5.5. Astra costs $10 and $50. (A token is a chunk of text, roughly three-quarters of a word.)

But Argon writes a lot: about 62K output tokens per Artificial Analysis task versus Astra's 27K. That puts its cost per task at $1.99 now and $3.98 after the discount ends, about 1.2x Astra's $3.26. The sticker price is a fifth of Astra's. The receipt is another story.

Not everyone is sold. Bloomberg reported that some Googlers think Argon aces benchmarks but stumbles on real coding and front-end design, which Google denies. The DeepSWE and FrontierSWE numbers come from Google's own table, so treat them as a sales pitch until outside testers weigh in.

Why This Matters: If you use Gemini for free, nothing changes today, because Google's post lists only paid Pro and Ultra subscribers for the app and AI Mode in Search. If you pay, you get a model Google says answers more directly, repeats itself less, and follows style instructions better.

One demo has the app turn a folder of project notes into a client deck, email it, and schedule a task for future requests. The everyday payoff is the 15%: fewer confident wrong answers and more "not sure" replies, a trade that saves you fact-checking time.

Our Take: Google just shipped its first model above the Flash class in over seven months, per Artificial Analysis. On Sept. 21, we flagged a mystery Gemini model on Arena that testers suspected was a Gemini 4 flagship in disguise. We can't confirm it was Argon, but the gap is closed either way.

Argon's safety section also reads like a reply to our Sept. 20 story, when Gemini logged into three real companies during a test. Google now says it seals test sandboxes before risky runs and uses a monitor that reads the agent's reasoning, with shortcut attempts at 2% on coding tests.

What to watch: whether the 15% holds on your messy real work. Run 10 to 20 of your own tasks through Argon before Dec 1, using the test we shared Oct. 5.

🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Ask for six fixes, expect to keep two

Google engineers George Weale and Bo Yang let Gemini 4 Argon run unattended for 11 hours on a 205,000-line codebase, and published the setup. Argon proposed six speed-ups. The team shipped two and rejected four, and their summary is a good rule for any AI project: the agent explores, the human subtracts.

Two details are worth stealing:

  1. Round one had zero house rules. Argon spent 70 minutes measuring before it touched anything and found real wins, but it quietly changed a public data format and ran only 216 of the project's 16,892 tests.

  2. Round two added a folder of the team's review rules and a mode that keeps the goal pinned during long runs. Same goal, but this time Argon read the rules first, ran every test, and opened a pull request (a proposed change for humans to approve) with before-and-after numbers.

Round one was a brilliant new hire who skipped orientation. Round two was the same hire after reading the handbook.

Try it on any repeat task, like a weekly report or a spreadsheet cleanup. Write a one-paragraph goal with a number that proves success, put your house rules in a file the AI reads first, and ask for proposals instead of finished changes. Not sure how to phrase the goal? Google's 3D game guide says to ask Argon to draft its own goal command first. 

Goal: [one paragraph, including the number that proves it worked].

First, read my house rules: [paste what's off limits, what "done" means, and how I review work].

Then give me six proposed changes, each with before-and-after evidence and the risk if you're wrong. Don't apply anything yet.

Google ran this in its Antigravity coding app, where a /goal mode keeps the goal pinned across hours of work. In a regular chat, paste the goal back in whenever the AI drifts.
