😸 Trump Named an AI Czar and Created a "Super Intelligence Force"

On Sunday, Trump tapped Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton as his AI czar (i.e., the single point person for AI policy) and put him in charge of a new task force called the Super Intelligence Force.

Yes, that is the real name. It follows last week's executive order telling federal agencies to say "Super Intelligence" instead of AI in public communications.

Here's what happened:

  • Trump announced the force on Truth Social, saying it will coordinate the government's dealings with consumers, public interest groups, religious organizations, critical infrastructure providers (e.g., power and water utilities), and AI companies.

  • Clayton will share leadership with FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson, Pentagon technology chief Emil Michael, and Office of Personnel Management Director Scott Kupor (the federal government's HR department).

  • The group reports to Trump and chief of staff Susie Wiles.

  • Clayton told the Wall Street Journal the force has 120 days to report on AI's risks and benefits and recommend what role Washington should play.

Why this matters: Last week's White House accord was a voluntary pledge that effectively left AI companies to police themselves. This force adds agencies with real levers: antitrust (the FTC), military contracts (the Pentagon), and the federal workforce (OPM).

Clayton has already shown his hand:

  • He argued against pausing AI development at U.S. companies, and said federal agencies, rather than civil courts, are best placed to police AI labs.

  • He told the Journal that falling behind is the bigger risk.

  • He said he does not want to work with Xi Jinping on governing AI.

The personal stakes: If agencies, rather than courts, end up refereeing AI companies, the rules for tools like ChatGPT and Claude get written in federal offices instead of courtrooms. Whatever lands in that 120-day report is the first draft.

Elon Musk is already on board. He confirmed on X that SpaceXAI (the division formed in July when SpaceX bought xAI) will be renamed SpaceXSI. SpaceXSI sounds like a crime procedural, and I would watch it.

Our take: The report is due in roughly four months, which is fast for Washington. The force will also consult religious organizations, which tracks with the week AI had (see the top of today's issue).

The open question: can a group whose stated priority is winning the race also write rules that slow it down? Ferguson still has an open FTC investigation into OpenAI and Anthropic, and now a second seat at the table where the rules get drafted. Watch how those two jobs overlap.

🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Test AI models on your own work before you switch

Flavio Copes has a reality check for every launch-day chart claiming a new model wins: none of them used your work. Even Anthropic's Opus 5.5 launch page, he notes, admits benchmark gaps (scores on standardized AI tests) are a less reliable guide to real-world differences. His fix: build a mini test from your own work.

  1. Gather 10 to 20 tasks you've really given an AI, each with an answer you know is right. Copes says twenty of these beat any public leaderboard.

  2. Run each task through both models, five times each, since answers change from run to run.

  3. Read every failure before trusting the score. One of Copes's models jumped from 75% to 87.5% after he fixed a grader that was too strict, not the model.

  4. For open-ended work like summaries, have a second AI grade against a short rubric (a checklist of what a good answer includes). Spot-check its verdicts.

  5. Compare cost per task, not price per token. Two models with identical token prices differed threefold in cost per task in his comparison.

Grader prompt: 

You are grading another AI's answer.

Task: [paste the task]
What a good answer must include: [3 to 5 specific points]
Answer to grade: [paste the answer]

Reply with PASS or FAIL, then one sentence on why.
