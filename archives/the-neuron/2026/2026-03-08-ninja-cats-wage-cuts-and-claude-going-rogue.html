The Real AI Threat to Your Career Isn't Job Losses. It's What Happens to Your Paycheck.

DEEP DIVE: What the data means for jobs (and actually, wages) going forward.

Here's a weird contradiction. Anthropic published a major labor market study this week showing AI could theoretically handle 94% of computer science tasks... but is only being used for 33%. There’s been no unemployment spike for highly exposed workers since late 2022. Meanwhile, Citadel Securities released a chart showing software engineering job postings are up 11% year-over-year.

Wait, so AI ISN’T taking jobs? Not so fast.

Buried in Anthropic's own data: hiring of young workers (ages 22-25) into AI-exposed occupations has dropped ~14%. A separate study found firms adopting GenAI reduce junior headcount entirely through slower hiring, not layoffs. And Block just cut nearly half its workforce, with Jack Dorsey explicitly saying AI lets smaller teams do the same work.

Here's what's actually happening:

  • Alex Imas' research documents clear micro-productivity gains (14-55%+ in coding, support, writing) that haven't shown up in macro statistics yet, mirroring past IT revolutions

  • AI makes software dramatically cheaper to build, so demand for software explodes (hence Citadel's rising job postings)

  • But each unit of software needs fewer people to build it

  • Imas and Shukla's adoption data shows AI usage is concentrated among higher-skilled, higher-income workers, and the gap isn't closing. Even 18 months after firms bought AI coding licenses, only half of engineers actually used them. The people who invest in learning gain leverage; the ones who don't fall behind.

  • Put all that together, and the labor pool you're competing with grows, because remote work already globalized the talent market, and AI amplifies that effect further

We posit the real issue isn't losing jobs. It's losing wages. More software jobs exist, but the price per unit of work is falling. Companies hire more people to build more things, but they don't need to pay each person as much when the tools do more of the heavy lifting. This is the pattern that played out with remote work (contractors competing with a global workforce on price); now add AI on top.

Why this matters: This is all why we keep pushing you to learn to code, to build with AI, to ship your own projects, and to learn to run your own models. Learning these skills is insurance. The ability to create your own value, independently, without relying on a single employer to pay you for tasks that get cheaper every quarter... that's the most durable career move you can make right now.

AI Skill of the Day

If you're new to Claude, you need to check out Ruben Hassid's viral Cowork guide. His original Claude guide broke down the six tools most people don't realize Claude has (Cowork, model selection, Excel, Plugins, Artifacts, and Projects). This new one goes deep on the most important one: Cowork.

The core idea: build a folder of .md text files (your writing style, your rules, your best past work) and point Claude Cowork to it. Now your prompts can be 10 words long and still sound like you.

Ruben's folder structure:

  • ABOUT ME/ ... who you are, what you do, your anti-AI-writing rules

  • PROJECTS/ ... one subfolder per active project with briefs and references

  • TEMPLATES/ ... your best finished work as reusable patterns

  • CLAUDE OUTPUTS/ ... where Cowork delivers finished files

Then use this one prompt for everything:

I want to [TASK] to [SUCCESS CRITERIA]. First, explore my folder. Then, ask me questions using the AskUserQuestion tool. I want to refine the approach with you before you execute.

The takeaway: One great .md file is worth more than 50 random uploads, and quality context beats clever prompting every time.
