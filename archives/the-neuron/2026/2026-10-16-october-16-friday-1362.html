😺 404 Media Ran Ads Inside ChatGPT to Show How Advertisers Can Target You

Want to make a point about ChatGPT privacy? Buy an ad inside ChatGPT. That's what 404 Media did, running two ads that read "ChatGPT isn't private. OpenAI hires humans to read users' chats. Learn more," aimed at people asking about privacy and AI. Trolling the chatbot from inside the chatbot. Respect.

The stunt matters less than the tool behind it. Reporter Joseph Cox's tests show advertisers can aim at you two ways: by what you're asking about right now, and by who you are.

Here's what 404 Media found:

  • Context hints: advertisers describe topics or keywords, and OpenAI says these "guide matching but aren't exact-match targeting rules." Think Google keyword ads, but looser.

  • Uploaded lists: advertisers upload emails, phone numbers, or Android advertising IDs, and OpenAI matches them to real ChatGPT accounts. Lists over 25,000 entries can target people. Smaller ones can only exclude them.

Uploads can be raw (readable emails) or hashed (scrambled into codes). OpenAI accepts either and didn't say whether it hashes raw uploads. Meta does. OpenAI says files are deleted after processing, usually within 24 hours, and advertisers can't see which individuals matched.

Then there's the off switch. You can turn off "ads personalization," but 404 Media says that doesn't stop ads or tracking entirely, and the setting says ads "may still be based on your current chat." So "off" means "less," not "none." Great.

Keep the scale in view, though. These are 404 Media's own tests, and customer-list targeting is standard across advertising. Zach Edwards of DecryptAds calls it "boiling the frog," and the article's estimate is that most of ChatGPT's roughly 1 billion monthly users don't know any of this.

Two things to watch: whether OpenAI answers the hashing question, and whether "off" ever covers your current chat. Until then, treat a ChatGPT conversation like a search box that can show ads.

🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Reconcile Messy Exports with the Four-Bucket Method

The Powerdrill team's guide to reconciling transactions (checking that two records of the same activity agree) opens with a scene every finance person knows. A card payment shows up once in your ledger, your own record of transactions, and twice in the processor's export, split into a charge and a fee. One invoice reads INV-0042 in one system and INV42 in the other. A transfer started on the 31st settles on the 1st and bumps the whole month.

Their fix: stop hunting for matches and start sorting leftovers. Every unmatched row falls into one of four buckets: missing on one side, duplicated, wrong amount, or right payment in the wrong period. Spreadsheet lookups stall because they assume one row on each side equals one event. They break when a single settlement covers forty transactions (a many-to-one match).

Powerdrill built this around its own tool, so the chatbot version below is our adaptation:

  1. Upload both exports and ask the chatbot to flag mismatched column names, date formats, and reference styles before matching anything.

  2. Ask for the four buckets by name, with a dollar tolerance and a few days of date slack. Then ask which groups of rows in one file add up to a single row in the other.

  3. Check row counts and unmatched dollar values, not just the grand total, since two equal errors in opposite directions still produce a perfect total.

Keep every row and add a status column instead of deleting matches, so you can explain a call six weeks later. Spot-check a few proposed groupings by hand.

Start with a month you already closed and see what the many-to-one question catches that your old lookups missed.
