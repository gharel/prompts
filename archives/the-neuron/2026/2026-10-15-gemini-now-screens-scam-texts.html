😸 Google Put Gemini on Scam-Text Duty in Messages, and You Have to Opt In

Google is expanding Gemini-powered scam detection in Android's Messages app with a new Enhanced Protection mode. When a number not in your contacts texts you, cloud-based Gemini models can read the conversation to judge whether it's a scam.

The sneakiest scams have no link at all. A University of Florida study of 175,430 user-reported scam texts found link-based scams (fake toll and postal notices) reuse near-identical scripts, which makes them easy to filter. Reply-based scams like romance and wrong-number texts adapt to each victim, and they grew much faster. A wrong-number text with no link gives a keyword filter nothing to chew on.

Here's what's new, per Google's post:

  • Cloud check: Gemini reads unknown-sender chats for scammer intent. Google says the messages stay private, even from Google (we couldn't verify that).

  • Link check: On-device Gemini Nano checks unfamiliar links using real-time web signals before you tap. It needs a Pixel 10 or newer, a Galaxy S26, or select devices.

The catch: it's opt-in and rolls out over the coming weeks. Google's post describes link warnings, not blocking, so the final call is still yours. It also covers only unknown senders, while the Florida study found scammers often move victims to WhatsApp or Telegram.

And the problem is big:

  • $470M: Text scam losses reported to the FTC in 2024, at minimum.

  • $15.9B: Total reported fraud losses in 2025, a record, with texting the top reported contact method.

  • 58%: How much likelier Android users were to report zero scam texts in a week, in a Google-released 2025 survey of US, India, and Brazil users (self-reported).

Why this matters: Scam texts are a numbers game, and catching the conversation before the money ask is where AI could genuinely help. If you're on a supported Android phone, flip this on when it arrives.

Our take: A good extra layer, not a fix. The smartest part targets scams that adapt, but the people most at risk are the least likely to find a settings toggle. Google also hasn't shared how often it flags real texts by mistake or misses scams. In the Florida team's test of general chatbots, GPT-5 missed 18.8% of scams. So how often will Gemini get it wrong?

🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Make a second AI attack your work (without letting it panic you)

Anthropic's merged code changes jumped from about 2,500 a day in May to over 30,000 a day by September. The company built its own replacement for Git (the standard tool for tracking code changes) to keep up. It says it makes extensive use of adversarial review, which means pointing an AI at finished work with the job of attacking it instead of approving it. Simon Willison says he uses a similar approach, often pitting Claude and GPT against each other.

The catch is why this is an advanced skill. Anthropic says the technique can turn myopic, meaning the reviewer fixates on the concern it was handed. In one case, a review got overly defensive about trusting another internal service, and Claude responded by piling on validation and complexity. Engineers often had to steer it away from over-building. Every office has that one reviewer who flags a one-in-a-million risk and derails the whole doc.

The post doesn't give a recipe, so this part is our adaptation. Run your draft (a contract, a financial model, a strategy memo) through a different model than the one that wrote it. Then make the reviewer rank problems by likelihood and cost, so you can see what to ignore. 

You are a skeptical reviewer. Below is [a contract / financial model / strategy memo] written for [audience and goal]. Find what could go wrong.

1. List at most 7 problems, ranked by likelihood times cost.
2. For each, say what would have to be true for it to matter, and how likely that is in [my real situation].
3. Separate real flaws from hypothetical risks, and flag anything you would advise me to ignore.
4. Do not suggest rewrites yet.
