A conversation with Inner Logic co-founder Mathias Unberath about autonomous surgery, soft-tissue robotics, and the simulation layer procedural devices need.

Welcome, humans.

What if every hospital could give patients access to the world’s best surgeon, performing at their absolute best, every single day?

That is the long-term promise of autonomous surgery. But before you picture a robot rolling into the operating room alone, our latest podcast episode explains what the technology can do today, what remains science fiction, and why progress looks more like a ladder than a giant leap.

Grant sat down with Mathias Unberath, CTO and co-founder of Inner Logic, formerly Semaphor, and an award-winning computer vision and simulation researcher at Johns Hopkins Whiting School of Engineering. Inner Logic just announced an $11.5M seed round co-led by General Catalyst and Bison Ventures to build the simulation and validation infrastructure surgical autonomy will need before it reaches patients.

No pressure. It only needs to work while someone is open on the table.

Here’s our favorite parts:

  • (20:52) The best surgeon on their best day: Mathias explains how an autonomous system could capture elite surgical skill and reproduce it consistently across hospitals.

  • (25:44) The “we’re too squishy” problem: Eyes and bones are relatively predictable. Livers, gallbladders, and other soft organs move and deform, forcing robots to adapt in real time.

  • (29:07) Robots fail in deeply unnatural ways: A machine can enter a situation no surgeon would create, so developers must generate mistakes and recovery scenarios that barely exist in real-world training data.

  • (33:41) The stroke treatment that cannot wait: Autonomous systems could bring specialized procedures to hospitals that lack the right expert when every minute affects a patient’s outcome.

  • (52:40) Surgery’s Waymo moment: One robotic failure might become a viral story, even when the technology produces safer outcomes across the entire population.

Why watch this? The episode covers what “autonomous” really means to the uncomfortable questions that arrive once machines become better at certain surgical tasks than people.

The conversation separates today’s robot-assisted surgery from the future vision of fully autonomous surgery. You will learn why autonomy is easier with bones than soft tissue, how robots learn from demonstrations, and where humans remain essential.
