😺 Google let patients talk to an AI before seeing a doctor

So Google tested its medical chatbot, AMIE, with 98 real patients before urgent-care appointments. The idea: let AI collect the story so your doctor can walk in knowing what's been going on.

Here's how it went:

  • Patients described their symptoms over text. AMIE asked follow-up questions, and doctors received the conversation and a summary before the appointment.

  • Google says the doctor's final diagnosis appeared on AMIE's list of possible diagnoses 90% of the time.

  • A physician monitored every chat and could step in. Supervisors spotted one hallucination and added clinical information in five conversations.

No conversation triggered the study's preset stopping rule. That's encouraging, though this trial had no comparison group and doesn't establish how AMIE would perform without supervision.

Good. Someone still needs to hear my 12-minute theory about whether the headache is related to that sandwich.

Then there's Utah, where the job gets bigger: The state has approved healthcare AI pilots that could eventually let some prescriptions reach patients before a clinician checks each one.

Take August AI: its planned pilot could let AI approve a 90-day refill without a clinician checking it first. Its state agreement allows review to move from checking every refill before delivery to checking afterward, then potentially just one in 20 per medication category.

The pilot hasn't started; each reduction needs state approval, and AI can only refill certain existing, non-controlled medications at the same dose.

Nolla Health's acne pilot is already active, with two physicians initially reviewing every prescription. Expect Fitness's approved pelvic-floor care pilot could eventually move to spot checks for lower-risk cases.

Elsewhere, AI is helping with the hospital to-do list: Healthleap scans adult patients' hospital records for malnutrition risk so dietitians know whom to assess.

Teladoc's SoloScribe drafts clinical notes during visits. Its SoloVitals feature estimates heart and breathing rates through a camera.

Why this matters: AI is getting closer to decisions that affect your care. Helping a doctor prepare is useful. Sending a prescription before a clinician checks it is asking for a lot more trust. I'd watch how often these systems miss warning signs as human review decreases. “We caught it” is much more reassuring before the prescription reaches you.

🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Spot what AI added to your summary

You give AI an article about a tool being tested. Its summary says the tool is available now. Somewhere between reading and writing, a test became a launch.

Ask AI to separate what the source actually says from its own interpretation: conclusions it drew while summarizing. Put each claim beside the original wording so you can see where the meaning changed.

  1. Paste the source text and AI's summary under separate headings.

  2. Run the prompt below, then check the quoted passages yourself. AI can mislabel its own work.

  3. Keep supported claims, clearly label useful interpretations, and correct or remove unsupported statements. 

Check this summary against the source text.

For each claim, show:
- The claim from the summary.
- The exact source passage and where to find it, or "no supporting passage."
- One label: STATED (the source says it), INTERPRETATION (a conclusion drawn from the source), or UNSUPPORTED (missing or contradictory evidence).
- Any wording that makes the claim stronger or more certain than the source.
