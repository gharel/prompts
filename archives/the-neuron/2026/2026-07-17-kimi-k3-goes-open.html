😼 Kimi K3 Shows Open Models Are Moving Upstairs

So there’s a new model from Chinese open weights AI company Moonshot that came out yesterday and it’s called Kimi K3. It arrived with 2.8 trillion parameters (the model's internal knobs for learning patterns), one million tokens of memory (roughly hundreds of thousands of words it can keep in view), and the oh so subtle energy of a new neighbor parking a battleship in the driveway.

Here's what happened:

  • Moonshot AI released Kimi K3, an open 3T-class model with native vision, a 1M-token context window, and full weights which are due to be released by July 27.

    • Open weights means the core model files can be downloaded, hosted, modified, and fine-tuned instead of only rented through an API.

  • Moonshot says K3 uses sparse experts (specialized sub-models that only partly activate for each task) to make a huge model cheaper to run than if every part switched on every time.

  • The early numbers are loud: K3 beat GPT-5.6 Sol on BrowseComp and Automation Bench in Moonshot's table, while Artificial Analysis said it used 21% fewer output tokens than Kimi K2.6.

  • The catch: Simon Willison found it capable but costly, with one SVG test burning more than 16,000 output tokens. Moonshot recommends 64 or more accelerators for serious deployment.

How to try it:

  • Use Kimi K3 on kimi.com, Kimi Code, or the Kimi API.

  • Watch for open weights by July 27 if your team wants to host or customize it.

  • Watch this hands-on Kimi K3 walkthrough for side-by-side design, 3D, and coding tests against Fable 5, plus the bigger argument that cheaper open intelligence changes distribution as much as capability.

Why this matters: Chinese AI companies like Z.ai and Moonshot who makes Kimi have been yipping at the heels of frontier quality models with every new release, so much ink will be spilled about that (and yes, it’s a big deal). But let’s point out something else:

Remember how Thinking Machines released Inkling yesterday? That was a 975B-parameter open-weights model built for customization through their Tinker tool. Thinking Machines says it used open models, including the older Kimi K2.5, to bootstrap some of Inkling's early post-training data. So Kimi is not only competing with U.S. labs. Its earlier models are already part of the supply chain for other US model companies. This is why open source is good!

So, before people who don’t know what they’re talking about in the government or Anthropic try to scare anyone into thinking otherwise, remember this: if a Chinese open model comes out that is near frontier quality, this is good for everyone, because global companies (including US ones!) get another powerful base layer they can adapt and run privately. This is why you don’t want to ban open weights Chinese models, government people. When China opens them, American companies who adopt them win. 

Our take: An added bonus for AI consumers: when a new frontier level open model comes out, U.S. labs lose some pricing and distribution leverage. This is also good for American companies whose engineering departments are like frogs about to be boiled alive when these companies go public.

Kimi K3 still has to prove itself outside launch-day demos and vendor benchmarks, and open does not magically mean free, as it still costs compute power to deploy and run them (this is why frontier intelligence models need not be just open, but small, too; we’ll get there).

Paired with Inkling, K3 points to the next fight: closed labs selling polished assistants versus open ecosystems selling control, customization, and compounding advantage. Which system do you want to build your company on top of?

🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Match the Agent to the Risk

Most AI mistakes start before the prompt: you give tiny and scary tasks the same scrutiny. Today’s skill is effort routing: deciding when an agent needs more context, effort, memory, or a second reviewer.

ClaudeDevs’ useful distinction: the model controls context and token cost; effort controls how hard Claude works: reading files, planning, verifying, or persisting. For code, Claude Code’s /code-review now runs low to ultra; /code-review ultra uses multi-agent cloud review to reproduce bugs before reporting them.

Use low effort for quick drafts and small diffs. Use high or ultra for auth, payments, data, launches, or anything painful to rollback. For recurring work, use memory/connectors like Mem0 for long-lived context and loops only with a stop rule. Addy Osmani’s reminder: judgment comes from reading outputs, logging mistakes, and building evals. 

Before you start, route this task:

1. Does this need context, effort, memory, or review?
2. Pick the smallest safe mode: quick, standard, deep, or ultra.
3. Define evidence for correctness.
4. Run it and list findings by confidence.
5. Log guesses, failed checks, and manual checks.
6. Stop if evidence is thin or risk exceeds the mode.
